How it is made

Method

What this is

Art Moves is a weekly brief on notable sales at Sotheby's, Christie's and Phillips, across fine art and other collectibles: watches, jewelry, cars, design and fashion. It is written for a reader who knows finance and is new to the art market.

Every issue asks three questions:

  • Who paid what? The price with and without fees, the estimate, any guarantee, and the buyer when it is on the record.
  • What did it fetch last time? What the same object sold for before, and how much it has gained or lost a year since.
  • Why would a careful person buy art? What the week's results mean if you are thinking about buying something yourself.

Sources

  • Prices and estimates come from the auction house's own results page or press release wherever possible. When a figure comes from the press instead, the row says so.
  • Buyer identity and context come from established art-market press, credited by name.
  • Every factual claim links to its source. Articles are summarized, not reproduced.

Images

  • Images of the works are found through web search and used to report on the sale. Some are auction-house photographs.
  • Each image carries a credit and links to the page it came from.
  • Where a work is in the public domain or openly licensed, the caption says so. Any other image may be in copyright and belongs to its rights holder.
  • Where no photograph of the exact lot can be found outside sothebys.com, the sale shows an illustration made with an AI image model instead. Illustrations are marked "Illustration" on the image and "AI-generated for Art Moves. Not the work sold" in the caption. They show the kind of object, not the object itself, and never copy a specific artwork, signature or brand mark.
  • Each image sits on a colour that matches its category: red for fine art, blue for watches, pink for jewelry, green for cars, amber for design, violet for anything else.

Evidence labels

  • Confirmed means the auction house or a primary document said it.
  • Reported means a named outlet said it, and the outlet is credited.

Buyers

  • When a credible outlet reports who bought a lot, the buyer is named and the report is credited.
  • Each named buyer links to a profile that explains who they are and where their money comes from.
  • Otherwise the buyer is listed as anonymous. What is on the record about the bidding is described; guesses about private individuals are not printed.

Prices

  • Each price states whether it is the hammer price or includes the buyer's premium, the fee the house adds on top of the hammer.
  • Estimates are published without fees. Where only a fee-inclusive result is available, the comparison with the estimate is flattering to the result, and the row is labelled.
  • Other currencies are converted to US dollars at the European Central Bank reference rate for the sale date.

Price history

  • Where the same object has changed hands before, its sale carries a price history: a chart of documented prices, the change since the last one, and every change of hands on the record, each with its source.
  • A price counts only if an auction house, a catalogue or a named outlet reported it. Private sales with no disclosed price appear as ticks on the time axis, without a number. Restitutions and inheritances are listed but are not sales.
  • The chart joins prices with a dashed line because nothing traded in between. It is not a price series.
  • Change and annualized figures compare fee-inclusive prices at both ends, in US dollars as reported at the time. They are nominal, and they leave out the seller's commission, insurance, storage and tax, so the owner's own return is lower.
  • Only works that come back to market get a second price. Works that did badly tend not to be resold, so repeat-sale returns flatter art as an asset (Korteweg, Kräussl and Verwijmeren, Review of Financial Studies, 2016).
  • No figure is estimated. Where there is no earlier public price, the history says so.

How it is made

Each Sunday an automated run in Perplexity Computer researches the week's results, adds them to the dataset, checks the numbers, writes the issue and publishes it once the checks pass. A human editor reads every issue after it goes out, and anything wrong is fixed with a dated correction at the foot of the issue. Code, data and text are kept under version control, so every change is on the record.

Sunday email

  • The sign-up form sends your email address to Buttondown, the service that delivers the email. You confirm once by email before anything is sent.
  • Your address is used for the Sunday email and nothing else. Every email carries an unsubscribe link.

Corrections

Corrections are made in the dataset and noted on the affected issue.
