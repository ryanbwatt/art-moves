## The problem

- A lot of art changes hands by inheritance, not by choice. The new owners often like the person who left it more than the work itself.
- Selling costs money twice. A commission is charged on the way out, and fees and premiums are charged again when the cash buys something new.
- Holding costs money too: insurance, storage and wall space for something nobody looks at.
- The aim would be to get a work to someone who wants it, at a fair value, and bring home something the owner would rather live with. Cash would be the fallback, not the point.

## A worked example

This is based on a real family. Personal details stay private.

- A couple in Canada inherited several paintings by Robert Pilot (1898 to 1967) from a grandmother. Pilot was a Montreal painter of Quebec streets, harbours and winters. They don't want to hang the paintings. They would either sell at market value or, better, trade them to someone who will.
- Pilot is well documented. MutualArt lists more than 500 auction results, with a record of $262,469 at Heffel in 2018 and an average of about $8,294 for paintings over the past 12 months ([MutualArt](https://www.mutualart.com/Artist/Robert-Pilot/B29CB2C37BF94FEB)). Recent results at Cowley Abbott run from CAD 9,600 to CAD 84,000 ([Cowley Abbott](https://cowleyabbott.ca/artist/robert_pilot/portfolio)).
- That is the profile this would be built for: real, documented, saleable works whose market is mostly domestic, held by owners who want an outcome rather than a windfall.

| Option | What the family gets | What it costs them |
|---|---|---|
| Sell at a Canadian auction | Cash at a public price | The seller's commission, and a wait for the right sale |
| Sell privately | Cash, possibly faster | Finding a buyer, and less price discovery |
| Trade through Art Moves (concept) | A work they would hang, plus or minus cash | Fees, shipping, insurance and a wait for a match |
| Donate to a museum | A good home and a possible tax receipt | No cash, and the museum has to want it |

**The cross-border catch.** Pilot lived in Canada and died more than 50 years ago. One of his paintings worth more than CAD 15,000 is on Canada's Cultural Property Export Control List ([Justice Laws](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/c.r.c.,_c._448/page-6.html)). Taking it out of Canada permanently needs an export permit, and the application can be referred to an expert examiner ([Canadian Heritage](https://www.canada.ca/en/canadian-heritage/services/export-permits-cultural-property/guide-exporting.html)). A trade with someone in New York would involve more than shipping.

## Who it would serve

| Owner | What they want | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| Heirs who don't want the work | A good outcome with little effort | Inventory, valuation, then a sale or donation |
| Collectors whose taste has changed | Something new without starting again in cash | A trade, or a trade with cash on one side |
| Heirs who like art but not this art | A collection that feels like theirs | A trade. This is the strongest case |

## How a trade would work

- **1. Submit.** Photographs of the front, back, signature and labels. Dimensions, condition, location, and every document that ties the work to its owner: bills of sale, estate papers, gallery invoices, exhibition labels, catalogue raisonné entries.
- **2. Review.** Art Moves would check the paperwork and decline anything it can't stand behind.
- **3. Price.** A range taken from documented comparable sales, using the same rules as the [price history](data.html) on the Data page. A range is not an offer.
- **4. Match.** A direct swap, a swap with cash on one side, or a sale followed by a purchase. A direct swap needs each side to want what the other has, so the cash options would keep the market moving.
- **5. Hold.** Both works go to a third-party fine-art storage facility. Each is unpacked, photographed and condition-reported on arrival.
- **6. Release.** Once both works are in and both owners accept the condition reports, both are released at the same time. If one side falls through, each work goes back to its owner.

## Custody: the lesson from The RealReal

The RealReal resells luxury goods on consignment. Its pitch is that it inspects every item, and that inspection is what makes buyers and sellers trust it.

- **What carries over.** Inspection is the product. The RealReal says more than a million items a month pass through its authentication centres, checked against records of tens of millions of authentic items ([The RealReal](https://www.therealreal.com/authentication)).
- **What it cost.** It had built up an accumulated deficit of about $1.3 billion by the end of 2025 ([10-K summary](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/REAL/10-k-the-real-real-inc-files-annual-report-7d721a7c41a5.html)). It reported its first operating profit, $1.5 million, in the third quarter of 2026 ([investor relations](https://investor.therealreal.com/financials/quarterly-results/default.aspx)).
- **What doesn't carry over.** A handbag can be checked against thousands of identical ones. A painting is unique. Its authenticity rests on the catalogue raisonné, the artist's foundation or estate, and named experts.
- **So:** rent space and handling from an existing fine-art storage firm instead of building a warehouse, and keep the volume small and documented.

## What Art Moves would check, and what it would not

| Would check | Would not claim |
|---|---|
| The documents match the object: title, dimensions, signature, labels | That a work is authentic. That opinion belongs to the catalogue raisonné authors, foundations and experts |
| The person submitting has the right to trade it, including executors acting for an estate | That a price range is what the work will fetch |
| Condition on arrival, photographed and agreed by both sides | Tax or legal advice for either party |
| Both works are in before either is released | |

## Money, fees and tax

- **Illustrative fee only:** 5% charged to each owner on the agreed value. On two works agreed at $15,000 each, that's $750 from each owner, $1,500 in total. Shipping, insurance and storage would be quoted separately.
- **A trade is not tax-free.** In the US, both sides of a barter must report the fair market value of what they receive, and organised barter exchanges must issue Form 1099-B ([IRS Topic 420](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc420)). Canadian tax treatment is not covered here.
- **No credits or guaranteed values.** Promising a trade-in value before a match is found means taking on inventory risk. That is a different business.

## Why it might not work

- **Each side has to want what the other has.** An algorithm can rank possible matches, but it can't make two owners want each other's work.
- **The wrong works would come in.** People offer what they can't sell, so the pool could fill with weak works and high expectations.
- **A price is not liquidity.** A comparable sale shows what someone once paid, not what someone will pay now.
- **Handling is the real cost.** Storage, condition reports, insurance and claims may cost more than the software.
- **Conflict of interest.** The editor works part-time with a commercial gallery. Any live service would need a written conflict policy first.

## What would need to be true

- Owners of documented works in roughly the $3,000 to $20,000 range say they would rather trade than take cash.
- A small number of trades clear after handling costs, with the fee accepted.
- Some owners go on to buy or trade into work they actually hang.
- The reasons failed matches fall through (taste, price, trust, shipping, timing) point to problems that can be fixed.
