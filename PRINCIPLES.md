# Art Moves: guiding principles

Added 13 Nov 2026. These sit on top of the rules already in `README.md`, `src/pages/about.md` and `WEEKLY_RUN.md` (sourcing, buyers, prices, images, publishing). Nothing here is built yet. Section 6 is the proposed build, waiting on the editor's approval.

## 1. The question every issue answers

**Why does a careful person buy art at all?**

The reader is not Ken Griffin. The reader is finance-literate, new to art, and counts every dollar: an investment-banking associate with a first purchase somewhere between $5,000 and $20,000. Every sale covered, every question asked of the reader and every suggestion made has to add to the answer.

The working answer, to be tested against each week's evidence:

| Reason to buy | What the evidence says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Financial return | Weak on its own. A broad portfolio of paintings returned 6.3% a year once the bias toward winners that get resold is removed (8.7% before), with a Sharpe ratio of 0.11. The sample is 32,928 paintings resold between 1960 and 2013, so it predates the current market. The authors' conclusion: a broad portfolio is not attractive, though specific styles or top-selling artists may add value. | [Korteweg, Kräussl and Verwijmeren, Review of Financial Studies, 2016](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/29/4/1007/1896045) |
| Cost of getting in | The buyer pays 28% on top of the hammer price up to $2M at both Christie's and Sotheby's, before tax, shipping, framing and insurance. A $10,000 hammer is a $12,800 bill. The seller pays a separate, negotiated commission on the way out. | [Christie's](https://www.christies.com/en/help/buying-guide-important-information/financial-information), [Sotheby's](https://help.sothebys.com/en/support/solutions/articles/44002686902-what-is-a-buyer-s-premium) |
| What wealthy buyers say | Asked for their single most important motive, 23% of 3,100 high-net-worth collectors chose financial investment (37% in the US). It was the top answer and still a minority. The rest chose pleasure and identity, social ties, a passion for collecting, family tradition, or supporting artists. | [Art Basel and UBS Survey of Global Collecting 2026](https://www.ubs.com/content/dam/assets/cc/about-ubs/contemporary-art/doc/survey-of-global-collecting-2026.pdf) |
| Trend | 59% of collectors cite a mix of passion and investment, down from 76% in 2014. | [Deloitte Art & Finance Report 2025](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-zone2/at/de/docs/presse/2025/deloitte-art-finance-report-2025.pdf) |

So the starting position: a careful buyer buys for use (living with it), identity, access to a world, and something to pass on, and treats holding value as a constraint, not the goal. "Do not lose much" is a reasonable target. "Beat the S&P" is not. Art Moves should say this plainly and revise it if the data says otherwise.

## 2. Read-through: what one sale means for the rest of the market

When a headline work sells, the issue connects three dots:

1. **The artist.** What the price does to that artist's own market.
2. **The peers.** What it may do to related artists.
3. **The way in.** Whether any of those peers is worth a careful buyer's attention, and how to buy one.

The model is equity read-through: Nvidia reports, AMD moves. The analogy is useful and has limits the reader should see.

| Where it holds | Where it breaks |
|---|---|
| Prices anchor. A painting's next price and its estimate are pulled toward its last auction price. ([Beggs and Graddy, American Economic Review, 2009](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/ark:/29072/ora_61de5b29b4094731a818b92d2c022985)) | Every work is unique and trades years apart. One sale is one data point, not a quote. |
| Peers move together, a little. One study of abstract artists at Sotheby's, 2000 to 2015, found a 10% rise in connected artists' prices went with a 2 to 3% rise in an artist's own. ([Peer effects in art prices, WU Vienna working paper](https://research.wu.ac.at/ws/files/37333635/WP298.pdf)) | That is roughly a quarter of the move, from a single working paper on one segment. Treat it as a hypothesis to check each week, not a rule. |
| A record changes what consignors and estimators expect. | A cheaper work by a peer is not a smaller position in the trophy. A print is a different asset from a painting. |

Rules for a read-through:

- **Key it to the sale, not the buyer.** Buyers are rarely named (2 of 34 in Issue 1, both intermediaries). The read-through has to work when the buyer is anonymous.
- **Peers are documented, not invented.** Each link has a type (teacher or pupil, same movement, same workshop, same dealer or estate, same period and region) and a source. Rembrandt's circle is his Leiden colleague Jan Lievens and his pupils Gerrit Dou, Govert Flinck and Ferdinand Bol ([Leiden Collection](https://www.theleidencollection.com/archives/essay/h._perry_chapman,_2017_rembrandt-and-the-leiden-collection_2023.pdf)), not Bruegel.
- **Say how strong the signal is.** Three labels: *observed* (peer results since the sale moved), *pattern* (it happened after earlier comparable sales, with the sales cited), *none yet*.
- **Say why the price moved**, with a source: supply (first work of its kind at auction in years), a guarantee, a museum show, restitution, a single determined bidder. If the reason is unknown, say so.
- **Research is the product.** The weekly run does this digging so neither the editor nor the reader has to.

## 3. Verdicts

The editor wants the issue to say whether a peer is a buy and how to buy it. Art Moves is opinionated, so it gives a view. Proposed form, pending approval:

- One of three: **Worth a look**, **Wait**, **Pass**. Each states its basis in one or two lines.
- Always shown with the all-in cost and what is known about resale: how often comparable works come to auction and what share sell.
- Never a price target or a predicted return. The About page says nothing on the site is investment advice, and that stays true.
- A verdict is about the work and the route, measured against the reader's stated budget and purpose. It is never advice to an individual.

## 4. Asking the reader

The reader does not know what they are looking for. The site asks a few broad questions and reshapes what it shows.

- Five questions at most. Plain words, no art terms. Every question has a "not sure" answer.
- Proposed questions: budget (all-in), when they want to buy, what form (painting, work on paper or print, photograph, sculpture or object, not sure), what matters most (living with it, holding value, a story, supporting a living artist), and a taste question answered by picking pictures, not naming styles.
- Each answer changes what is shown, and the page says why: "You said under $20,000 and a painting. That rules out X; here is what it leaves."
- Each tailored result still answers Section 1: why a careful person would or would not buy this.
- Answers stay in the reader's browser. No account, no tracking, no new service.

## 5. Guardrails carried over

- Motives of named people are printed only when reported or stated by them. Otherwise the issue describes what the structure of the sale shows (guarantee, estimate, bidding), not what a private person was thinking.
- Every figure in a read-through, a route or a verdict traces to a source, as in the sales data.
- Images in the taste question are public domain or openly licensed, with credit.
- The gallery disclosure applies with more force once the site points readers toward things to buy. If gallery works are ever suggested, each one is labelled.
